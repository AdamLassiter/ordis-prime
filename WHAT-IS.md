# What is Warframe, really?

I like *Warframe*, possibly too much.
The new player experience then was *fine*, but I never really got what the game's identity was supposed to be.
Why did I want to complete map nodes,though?
What was the story behind this warframe in a cryo-pod?
How come that weird human settlement on Earth such a pain to navigate and actually find the tiny sample of interactive NPCs?
I first played in 2018, but it never really clicked for me.
I picked up Frost Prime from the old Twitch x Amazon Prime rewards system a little later, and that became my main frame for a while because "He's *prime*, that must be better than everything else... right?"
From what little I remember of the game back then, I can only recall these weird things called "Void Fissures" that seemed like a short-term event at the time, given the rate they were dropping these cool-looking Prime parts, but I quickly burned through my meager collection of relics.
Alas, it was never meant to be; it wasn't until I revisited the game much later in 2025 that I had *that moment*.

At a glance, *Warframe* is an over-the-shoulder PvE action hero-shooter by Digital Extremes.
You begin as this strange fellow with a sword and a bow; I saw space-ninja and I leaned into that.
My beloved Paris carried me for longer than it should have done as I wandered through the solar system, surrounded by all these strange alien lifeforms.
The story takes a long time to get going, but I started to have this crashing realisation that this is far more than some generic sci-fi backdrop; all these loose story ends start to tie together.

Then, much later, I discovered the wall that is "The Steel Path".
You are *given* a flimsy combat suit, equip a handful of relics from a bygone feudal age, and carefully navigate a maze of sensors and alarms with enough cautious precision to make even a amateur burglar question your credentials.
You *choose* a biomechanical space warrior, equip an implausible number of weapons, and pinball through missions with enough speed and spectacle to make gravity feel more like a polite suggestion.
This machine of war-crimes and devestation is born out of this struggle against the relentless resilience that Steel Path throws at you, and only then does the game start to explain itself.

That is the surface answer, at least.
It is also the least complete one.

For some, it is a game about movement.
Bullet-jumping, aim-gliding and sliding turn an ordinary corridor into a playground, and a mission can feel good before the rewards screen has anything to say about it.
The destination may as well be just another checkbox on the todo list; the pleasure is arriving like a caffeinated meteor.

For some players, *Warframe* is a collectathon in the grand tradition of *Pokémon*.
Warframes, weapons, companions and stranger things besides all wait to be found, built and mastered.
Mastery Rank gives that appetite a number: not quite a score, not quite a level, but a very satisfying answer to “how much of this enormous medicine cabinet have I sampled?”

For others, the game is *Diablo* with bullet jumps: a damage laboratory.
A weapon is only the beginning; mods, Arcanes, abilities, companions and faction quirks turn it into an experiment.
These players are not merely defeating an enemy.
They are testing whether an interaction works, whether one more multiplier fits, and whether a perfectly ordinary sidearm can be persuaded to commit crimes against mathematics.
Some chase level-cap endurance or shave seconds from a specialised hunt.
Others pose a sillier problem: can this neglected weapon, themed loadout or deeply inadvisable Mod combination be made good?
The point is not always maximum power.
Sometimes it is making an idea work.

It can still be a space-ninja game, too.
The fact that a squadmate may delete the room with a glowing cannon does not stop another player from slipping past guards, cracking every cipher and leaving no evidence but several mysteriously unconscious bodies.
Warframe supports power fantasy, but it also supports patience, control and the quiet pleasure of doing a job cleanly.

It is also an unusually well-stocked theme park.
There is spaceship combat, fishing, mining, hoverboarding, decorating, conservation, roguelike excursions and several other activities that sound as though they wandered in from different games.
That can be charming or bewildering, often both within the same hour.
Some Tenno sample every attraction; others know exactly which rides they intend to ignore forever.

Then there are the Tenno who arrive for the action and stay for the people.
The Solar System is full of factions, histories, domestic spaces, unlikely friends and, eventually, romance.
To them, *Warframe* may be an ongoing science-fiction story, a lore excavation, or, yes, a dating simulator that happens to contain several hundred guns.

And naturally, fashion is the true endgame, although “fashion” may mean more than matching armour.
Some Tenno stage Captura photography, turn Dojos into impossible architecture, furnish their Orbiters, pursue the perfect companion, or just compose absent-mindedly on the Shawzin and Mandachord.
Colour palettes, armour pieces, Syandanas and carefully coordinated accessories can absorb as much thought as any combat build.
Saving the Origin System is important, but it is no excuse for clashing metallic accents, nor for leaving the resulting trophies in a heap.

For many people it settles into the comfortable rhythm of *Old School RuneScape*: choose a distant personal goal, consult a wiki and make numbers rise while a podcast plays.
The repetition can be the point, not an obstacle on the way to it.
Friends use it as a hangout.
Veterans make mentoring their endgame: teaching bewildered newcomers, generously trading their spare useful gear for a handful of fish and helping with the farm without simply playing the game for them.
Absent Tenno come back after a year and discover that someone has installed another theme park behind the old one.
The grind can be a satisfying ritual, or, when a desired part refuses to drop, a persuasive invitation to play something else for a while.

There is a useful way to name this sprawl.
In [*Why It's Rude to Suck at Warcraft*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BKP1I7IocYU), Dan Olson contrasts free play, or *paidia*, where curiosity, expression and doing something because the doing is delightful are ends in themselves, with instrumental play, or *ludus*, where a player chooses a measurable goal, learns the machinery and pursues it efficiently.
*Warframe* is full of both.
The danger is not optimisation; it is allowing one style to declare itself the only respectable way to play.

None of these players is misunderstanding the game.
Games give us goals, but players decide which goals matter.
One person wants completion.
Another wants mastery of a system, self-expression, a shared weekly ritual, a difficult test, or simply twenty minutes of glorious noise after work.
Most players move between several of these motives, sometimes in the same evening.

That freedom has a sharp edge.
Warframe is vast, layered and not always eager to explain itself.
A player can have a hundred possible goals and no clear idea which one belongs to them.
Research becomes part of play: comparing builds, following recipe chains and piecing together information on the second screen.
The game offers choices far faster than it offers context.

That variety is also why a Warframe catalogue is useful.
Collectors need to know what they are missing and where it comes from.
Build experimenters want to compare equipment, abilities and Mods without holding the whole game in their heads.
Challenge runners want reliable mechanics and exact comparisons before they spend hours refining a hunt or endurance build.
Progression-minded players want to turn a distant goal into a practical route through recipes, resources and missions.
Some players crack Relics for treasure; others discover that they have accidentally become small-business owners with a spreadsheet.
Traders and fashion-conscious Tenno have their own questions about value, availability and the exact name of that one piece they saw in a relay.

Ordis Prime is for the curious: *What is this?*
*How do I get it?*
*What works with it?*
*What should I do next?*
It connects the game's items, systems and sources without prescribing one “correct” way to play.
It helps you find a route through whichever version of *Warframe* you have chosen today.
